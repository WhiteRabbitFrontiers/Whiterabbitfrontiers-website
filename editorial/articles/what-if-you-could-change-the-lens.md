The right song can change the whole drive home. Same traffic, same errands—but zoom! The internal landscape shifts. You feel more animated. The day looks brighter.

What if you could change how the world feels? What would you dial up?

## Now imagine the settings menu

Imagine exploring a city with an artist’s attention, noticing the patterns beneath the bustle while the sensory overload fades into the background. Familiar streets reveal something unexpected—like seeing an additional color that was there all along, just beyond your perception.

Would you try it? Would your original setting eventually feel outgrown as you settled into the new landscape? Or would your favorite setting make you easier to please?

## We’re already learning to adjust the settings

**Pleasure.** In a clinical trial, therapy that helped people anticipate, notice, and savor rewarding experiences improved aspects of reward sensitivity and clinical symptoms more than a comparison therapy. What if familiar joys could feel rewarding again? [1]

**Pain.** In a trial involving primary chronic back pain, helping patients reinterpret pain as less threatening substantially reduced it for many. The pain was real. Changing its meaning could change how much it hurt. [2]

**Neurofeedback.** In a small depression trial, people practiced recalling happy memories while watching feedback from their own brain activity. Those receiving targeted feedback showed greater improvements in symptoms and access to specific positive memories than the control group. Follow-up was short. [3]

These treatments have limits. They don’t establish a universal settings menu. But they open a remarkable question: **How much of our internal landscape could we learn to change?**

## Who gets the controls?

Suppose a future tool could make work feel less exhausting. Would it help you recover—or make intolerable conditions easier to tolerate?

Could making a difficult conversation feel less threatening help you listen—or make you overlook something you needed to hear?

Discomfort carries information. So does delight. Before changing either, we would want to know what changes, whether we can undo it, and whose interests it serves.

Maybe you would dial up wonder. Maybe quiet. Maybe you would leave everything alone.

**What would you dial up—and what would you refuse to lose?**

## Follow the evidence

- 1. Craske and colleagues (2023). [“Positive affect treatment targets reward sensitivity: A randomized controlled trial.”](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10213148/) *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology.* Eighty-five adults received 15 weekly sessions. Positive Affect Treatment outperformed a therapy focused on negative affect on combined clinical outcomes and some reward measures; reward learning did not differ.
- 2. Ashar and colleagues (2022; published online 2021). [“Effect of Pain Reprocessing Therapy vs Placebo and Usual Care for Patients With Chronic Back Pain: A Randomized Clinical Trial.”](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34586357/) *JAMA Psychiatry.* A 151-person trial in primary chronic back pain, which was not attributed to a clear peripheral cause. Findings do not generalize to all pain or remove the need to assess injury and disease.
- 3. Young and colleagues (2017). [“Randomized Clinical Trial of Real-Time fMRI Amygdala Neurofeedback for Major Depressive Disorder: Effects on Symptoms and Autobiographical Memory Recall.”](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28407727/) *American Journal of Psychiatry.* Thirty-six unmedicated adults; two training sessions; assessment one week after the final session. Promising preliminary evidence, not proof of lasting benefit or general mood control.
